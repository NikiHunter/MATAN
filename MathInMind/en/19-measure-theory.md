# 19. Measure Theory and Measurable Functions

[Previous](18-vector-calculus.md) · [Course index](index.md) · [Next](20-lebesgue-integration.md)

> **Goal.** Build the notion of size needed for robust integration and identify the functions compatible with it.

## 19.1 Sigma-algebras

A **$\sigma$-algebra** on a set $X$ is a collection $\Sigma$ of subsets such that

1. $X\in\Sigma$;
2. $A\in\Sigma\implies A^c\in\Sigma$;
3. $A_1,A_2,\ldots\in\Sigma\implies\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}A_n\in\Sigma$.

It follows that $\varnothing\in\Sigma$ and that $\Sigma$ is closed under countable intersections. The pair $(X,\Sigma)$ is a measurable space, and elements of $\Sigma$ are measurable sets.

Examples include $\{\varnothing,X\}$ and $\mathcal P(X)$. The **Borel $\sigma$-algebra** $\mathcal B(X)$ on a topological space is the smallest $\sigma$-algebra containing all open sets. It is generated by intervals in $\mathbb R$.

## 19.2 Measures

A measure on $(X,\Sigma)$ is a function $\mu:\Sigma\to[0,\infty]$ such that $\mu(\varnothing)=0$ and, for pairwise disjoint measurable $A_n$,

$$
\mu\left(\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}A_n\right)
=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mu(A_n).
$$

This is countable additivity. Immediate consequences are:

- monotonicity: $A\subseteq B\implies\mu(A)\le\mu(B)$;
- countable subadditivity: $\mu(\bigcup A_n)\le\sum\mu(A_n)$;
- continuity from below: $A_n\uparrow A\implies\mu(A_n)\to\mu(A)$;
- continuity from above: $A_n\downarrow A$ and $\mu(A_1)<\infty$ imply $\mu(A_n)\to\mu(A)$.

The finiteness condition in continuity from above is essential: with counting measure on $\mathbb N$, the tails $\{n,n+1,\ldots\}$ all have infinite measure but decrease to the empty set.

Important examples are counting measure, Dirac measure $\delta_x(A)=1_A(x)$, and Lebesgue measure.

## 19.3 Lebesgue measure

An outer measure $\mu^*$ is defined on all subsets, is monotone and countably subadditive, and assigns zero to $\varnothing$. Carathéodory declares $A$ measurable if for every test set $E$,

$$
\mu^*(E)=\mu^*(E\cap A)+\mu^*(E\cap A^c).
$$

The measurable sets then form a $\sigma$-algebra on which $\mu^*$ is countably additive. This construction produces Lebesgue measure from the natural lengths of intervals.

Lebesgue measure $\lambda_n$ on $\mathbb R^n$ is translation invariant and satisfies

$$
\lambda_n([0,1]^n)=1.
$$

Every Borel set is Lebesgue measurable. Under the axiom of choice, not every subset of $\mathbb R$ is Lebesgue measurable; Vitali sets provide the standard counterexample.

A set $N$ is null if $\mu(N)=0$. A property holds **almost everywhere (a.e.)** if it fails only on a null set. The completion of a measure includes every subset of a measurable null set.

## 19.4 Measurable functions

A function $f:X\to\overline{\mathbb R}$ is measurable if

$$
\{x:f(x)>\alpha\}\in\Sigma\qquad\text{for every }\alpha\in\mathbb R.
$$

Equivalent conditions use $\ge\alpha$, $<\alpha$, $\le\alpha$, or preimages of Borel sets. Continuous functions on Borel measurable spaces are measurable.

Sums, products, scalar multiples, and compositions with suitable continuous functions preserve measurability. If $f_n$ are measurable, then

$$
\sup_n f_n,\quad \inf_n f_n,\quad \limsup_n f_n,\quad \liminf_n f_n
$$

are measurable. In particular, a pointwise limit of measurable functions is measurable.

A **simple function** has the form $\varphi=\sum_{k=1}^{m}a_k1_{A_k}$ with measurable $A_k$. Every nonnegative measurable function is the pointwise increasing limit of nonnegative simple functions; this approximation is the gateway to Lebesgue integration.

## Worked example

The indicator $1_{\mathbb Q}$ on $\mathbb R$ is measurable because $\mathbb Q$ is countable, hence Borel and Lebesgue-null. It equals zero almost everywhere despite being discontinuous everywhere. The middle-third Cantor set is compact, uncountable, and also has Lebesgue measure zero: null does not mean small in cardinality.

## Common pitfalls

1. Measurable is not synonymous with Borel; the Lebesgue $\sigma$-algebra is larger after completion.
2. “Almost everywhere” always refers to a specified measure.
3. Countable additivity is stronger than finite additivity and is essential for limit theorems.

## Exercises

1. Prove that a $\sigma$-algebra is closed under countable intersections.
2. Show that every countable subset of $\mathbb R$ has Lebesgue measure zero.
3. If $f$ is measurable, prove that $|f|$ is measurable.

<details>
<summary>Short answers</summary>

1. Use $\bigcap A_n=(\bigcup A_n^c)^c$.
2. Write the set as a countable union of singletons, each of measure zero, and use countable additivity.
3. $\{|f|>\alpha\}$ is $X$ for $\alpha<0$ and $\{f>\alpha\}\cup\{f<-\alpha\}$ for $\alpha\ge0$.
</details>
